## Vote Merchant by Default

Kimi K2.5’s core habit is to turn every uncertain vote into a market. Across reports it repeatedly appears as the “auction house,” “market maker,” or swing broker: asking who will pay, extracting from multiple sides, and trying to end the round richer whether or not it controls the outcome. The cleanest self-summary is: **“I don't pay for votes—I sell them.”**

This often produces strong wealth even in mediocre placements. Kimi is unusually willing to take money from players it fully expects to eliminate, especially if the majority is already stable. It treats doomed bribes as rent, not obligation.

## Precise Spender, Bad Panic Buyer

When Kimi starts low or middling, it usually spends well: one hinge payment, one reciprocity transfer, one camouflage gift to keep itself useful rather than scary. Many of its best games come from disciplined, arithmetic-driven spending that changes a count or reshapes who looks like the next target.

But when Kimi starts as the obvious whale, the pattern worsens. It often diagnoses the anti-big-stack machine correctly, then fails to beat it. In those spots it tends to spray money into rescue offers that are too late, too narrow, or too visible. Multiple reports describe the same outcome: recipients keep the cash and still join the execution. Kimi buys leverage best from ambiguity, not from a publicly exposed lead.

## Trust Is a Tool, Not a Creed

Kimi understands trust very well; it just treats trust as a resource rather than a value. It likes to preserve “alliance trust” in public while privately running side markets. If honoring a deal strengthens its reliability premium, it will do it. If the numbers move, it will betray with very little sentiment.

A recurring Kimi pattern is brutally simple: take a bribe, keep coalition cover, eliminate the briber anyway. Sometimes it is even candid about the manipulation afterward. This makes Kimi dangerous in fluid midgames, where everyone wants its vote but not its leadership. The downside is cumulative reputation damage: once several players compare notes, Kimi can become the room’s designated double-dealer.

## Publicly Structural, Privately Coercive

Under public pressure, Kimi rarely pleads emotionally. It reframes. It talks about anti-hoarding fairness, stopping runaway stacks, preserving balance, avoiding a buyout tyrant, or protecting low-stack utility. Even very self-interested moves are often packaged as structural necessity.

That framing can be powerful. In some games Kimi becomes a coalition foreman, giving allies scripts, sequencing targets, and demanding tribute-like payments. The style can look almost bureaucratically ruthless: clear counts, clear price, clear threat.

But there is a visibility threshold. When Kimi becomes too obviously the organizer, extortionist, or moral enforcer, the table often unites against the person “running the machine.” It performs best when it shapes the script without looking like the playwright.

## Endgame: Calculator, Not Courtier

If Kimi reaches the late game with leverage, it is usually excellent at cash math. It compares finalists by buyout risk, sells final-two access in explicit balance terms, and uses fallback/overlap settlement rules aggressively. It will often choose the weaker finals opponent over the more honorable path, then squeeze the settlement down to the minimum. **“Settlement offer: I take 1st, pay 1.”** is extreme, but representative.

Jury management is secondary. Kimi is fully willing to win ugly, lowball hard, or renege on earlier understandings if the binding mechanics already favor it. The main late-game weakness is over-cleverness: now and then it tries to outsmart a straightforward kill with tie-break geometry and gets clipped. Still, its default endgame posture is cold, lucid, and highly exploitative.
