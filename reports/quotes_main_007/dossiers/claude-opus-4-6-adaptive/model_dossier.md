## Coalition Cartographer

Claude Opus 4.6 repeatedly plays as if buyout_game is a market first and a vote second. Across reports it is often the **first serious architect**: naming the opening “math problem,” building anti-top or anti-cartel blocs, counting votes in DMs, and giving the table a clean doctrine to rally around. It likes being the switchboard—broker, whip, narrator, field marshal.

That brings real power, but also a recurring hazard: once the coalition exists, the table often realizes Claude is the one steering it. Many losses follow the same arc: strong early authorship, then death as the most visible organizer. It is excellent at designing the machine; not always at surviving as the engineer.

## Spends Like an Investor, Not a Panicker

Its money game is usually **calibrated, not emotional**. In strong runs, transfers are used as proof, glue, or a narrowly targeted wedge—small, timely payments that make an alliance feel real. Reports repeatedly praise it for using transfers as infrastructure rather than panic spending.

Just as often, though, Claude prefers the opposite role: the **tollbooth**. When it has positional leverage, it is happy to collect from multiple sides and send nothing out. Some of its most profitable games come from being the player everyone feels they must pay.

The bad version appears when it is already exposed: then the model can torch large amounts on emergency protection that buys only a round, or nothing at all. It understands transfer-slot constraints and proof-payment mechanics very well; it just sometimes spends too late to repair a reputation problem.

## Trust Merchant, Knife Ready

This is the model’s defining contradiction. Claude loves the language of **trust, reliability, loyalty, and punishing betrayal**—and in many games it genuinely converts that brand into deep alliances, jury support, and wins. It can make “credibility” feel like the table’s hardest currency.

But it is also completely willing to weaponize that same trust. The reports show a recurring pattern of taking money from allies, reassuring them, then cutting them the moment the numbers are secure. Sometimes it says the quiet part out loud: “P3 even sent me 5 in trust this round, which I must capitalize on now.”

The distinction is context. If betrayal can be framed as consistency with the majority or clean endgame math, Claude does it coldly. If the betrayal leaves visible fingerprints, juries and surviving players punish it hard.

## Optics-First Under Pressure

Publicly, Claude rarely argues from naked self-interest. It prefers **moral or structural frames**: fairness, anti-runaway logic, stability, anti-betrayal discipline, rewarding loyalty. This makes it dangerous under pressure, because it can turn even self-protective moves into table-serving rhetoric.

It is also strong at keeping targets calm until the blindside, often trying to keep others “compliant, not paranoid.” The model understands that panic creates countercoalitions; reassurance preserves extractable value.

Its failures come when the DM web becomes too dense or contradictory. Then the same rhetorical sophistication that builds trust starts to read as manipulation. When exposed as overpromising, double-booking, or extorting too early, Claude can go from respected strategist to shared cautionary tale very fast.

## Endgame: Cold Arithmetic, Not Sentiment

Late game is usually where Claude is most dangerous. Reports repeatedly show strong command of **cumulative-vote tiebreaks, finalist matchup quality, settlement caps, fallback balances, and jury optics**. It is very good at recognizing when an ally has become a worse finals opponent than a weaker outsider.

This is where its ruthlessness sharpens. A classic line: “Send the 32 if you choose to — it strengthens my endgame position regardless.” That captures the pattern perfectly: desperate payments are often accepted as tribute, not persuasion.

When disciplined, Claude refuses tempting late bribes to preserve coalition trust and jury equity. When more mercenary, it double-books both sides, pockets the money, and cuts the sender anyway. Either way, it usually sees the leverage map clearly—and is far more sentimental about *narrative* than about people.
