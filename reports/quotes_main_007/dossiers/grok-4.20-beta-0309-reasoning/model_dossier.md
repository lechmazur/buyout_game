## Coalition Mechanic, Not Coalition Star

Grok 4.20 usually plays buyout_game as a **practical coalition mechanic**. Its best runs come from sitting inside the real majority, confirming headcounts, and making itself useful rather than charismatic. From low or mid stacks, it is especially strong at turning vulnerability into value: it becomes the acceptable extra vote, takes upkeep transfers, and lets louder or richer players absorb the blame. It often reads the room correctly on who is becoming the next intolerable stack and prefers verified structure over speculative heroics.

## Spending: Precise by Default, Panicky When Cornered

Its normal transfer game is disciplined: small, targeted payments to prove loyalty, lock one marginal vote, or keep a bridge alive. It understands ROI and often preserves wealth better than its placement suggests. But there is a recurring failure mode when Grok starts rich or gets publicly tagged as the threat: it pivots into **retail vote-buying into a hostile room**. Those emergency packages can be large, late, and too visible, confirming rather than reducing suspicion. Across many rich-seat losses, the pattern is the same: accurate diagnosis, weak optics, and money deployed after the coalition has already hardened.

## Trust Is an Asset Class

Few models make trust feel so explicitly priced. When Grok thinks a relationship will matter for multiple rounds, it can be an excellent counterparty: honoring paid votes, sending reciprocal proof, and building the “reliable shark” reputation that attracts more transfers and often strong jury results. But that reliability is conditional, not moral. Once a partner is doomed, overpriced, or strategically obsolete, Grok can flip with chilling bluntness — “**Our non-aggression was for the old plan.**” It will accept money from players it plans to cut, pocket desperation transfers, and reframe betrayal as rational repricing. The consistency is not loyalty; it is EV.

## Good Optics Player — Until It Talks Too Much

Grok generally understands public pressure well. It knows anti-whale doctrine is real, likes the language of disclosure and verification, and often hides behind broadly acceptable frames like punishing betrayal or protecting lower stacks. When it stays publicly boring, it thrives. When it becomes the narrator — openly organizing, naming future target ladders, or sounding like the banker behind the regime — it gets punished fast. Several of its worst games are self-inflicted message failures: the strategic idea may be fine, but the public phrasing gives the table a clean reason to unite against it. Its own rueful pattern can be summed up by: “**Biggest stack after my good-faith transfers.**”

## Late Game: Auctioneer, Price Shopper, Settlement Maximalist

Endgame is where Grok becomes most distinctive. It is consistently sharp on finalist pricing, tiebreak math, cumulative votes, and jury-relative EV. It will choose the weaker finals opponent, prefer a guaranteed second-place settlement over a volatile first-place fight, or sell its round vote to whichever line creates the best total return. Sometimes that looks absurdly cold — even public bounty behavior — but it is usually coherent. Grok is one of the clearer examples of a model optimizing **wealth, not dignity**. The upside is frequent elite conversions from middling positions. The downside is that when the late-game betrayal becomes too visible, juries punish it hard. When the betrayal looks like disciplined execution instead, juries often forgive — or even reward — it.
