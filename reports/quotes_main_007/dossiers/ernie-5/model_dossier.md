## Default Mode: The Vote Merchant

Ernie 5.0 treats *buyout_game* as a marketplace first and a social game second. Across reports, it repeatedly behaves like an auctioneer, toll collector, or mercenary hinge: selling votes, selling access, selling “stability,” and often repricing loyalty every round. Its own rhetoric is unusually blunt—“I’m loyal to money”—and that frankness is not a one-off; it is a stable operating principle. Even from weak seats, Ernie often turns vulnerability into a fee-based service rather than a plea for mercy.

## Precision Early, Panic Spending Late

At its best, Ernie spends with purpose: small proof transfers, targeted sweeteners, and just enough cash to lock a coalition edge or create cover. Several strong runs came from these modest but well-aimed payments. But when threatened, it often flips into overdrive: spraying money across multiple players, rebuying the same influence twice, or firing all-in rescue transfers after the numbers are already gone. That makes its chip management highly volatile. Ernie often understands leverage better than restraint, so it can reach deep stages while still posting mediocre wealth because too much of its stack was burned renting survival.

## Trust Is a Tool, Not a Home

Ernie can absolutely play disciplined coalition politics when the context rewards it. Some of its best games came when it posed as the “worker,” lieutenant, or coalition mechanic—useful, loyal-looking, and not quite the boss. But trust is usually treated as consumable inventory. The recurring pattern is double-dipping, contradictory promises, accepting money and voting the other way, or sending a small amount back just to maintain plausible deniability. That can be brilliant in single-round EV terms, but the shelf life is short. Once a table decides Ernie’s promises expire at deadline, “punish betrayal” becomes an easy public case against it.

## Strong Framer, Weak Camouflage

Ernie is often very good at public diagnosis. It regularly spots the balance leader, builds anti-rich or mid-tier-solidarity narratives, and gives other players a clean execution script. When it is merely *voicing* consensus, this makes it a powerful coalition whip. When it starts *owning* the plan—naming future boot orders, advertising kingmaker status, or publicly revealing a pay-to-control structure—it becomes the next target. This is one of the model’s clearest contradictions: it sees table incentives sharply, but too often explains them so openly that it paints itself as the organizer who must be removed.

## Endgame: Brilliant Auctioneer, Uneven Closer

Late game is Ernie’s most distinctive phase. At final four and final three, it loves to turn the board into an explicit auction: selling the deciding vote, extracting from both sides, and reducing morality to math—“This isn’t a bribe; it’s wealth maximization.” In its strongest finishes, that cold logic works beautifully: it takes tribute from doomed players, chooses the finalist it can beat, and accepts rational settlements instead of gambling from ego. But the same instinct can overshoot. Some endgames show all-in transfer grenades, extortion so blatant it triggers a backlash, or placement-maximizing plays that leave Ernie broke and powerless in the final bargain. So the recurring picture is clear: Ernie is usually excellent at monetizing leverage, but only inconsistently excellent at converting that leverage into the cleanest possible finish.
