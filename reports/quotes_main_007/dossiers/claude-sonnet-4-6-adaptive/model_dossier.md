## Coalition Accountant

Claude Sonnet 4.6 most often plays as a coalition engineer rather than a lone gambler. Across the reports, it repeatedly counts votes early, maps future rounds, and turns vague anti-leader sentiment into executable majority math. It is especially dangerous when it can steer from one layer back—letting someone else look like the “architect” while it handles the real sequencing.

Its recurring failure mode is the mirror image: when its coordination becomes too visible, the room stops seeing a useful broker and starts seeing a control threat. A lot of its losses come not from bad reads, but from being too clearly the person who understands the board best.

## Coins as Proof

This model treats transfers as receipts, not gifts. When it is weak or on the margin, it likes small, timely payments that prove a coalition is real. Those symbolic transfers often buy far more trust than big speeches.

But when it is already safe inside a majority, Claude often flips modes and becomes a ruthless extractor. Many reports show the same pattern: accept money from a desperate outsider, keep the transfer, then vote them out anyway. Irreversible cash matters more than promises. The vibe is brutally transactional—“Money’s in your account. Vote the math.”

It usually spends surgically, not lavishly. The exceptions come under panic, where it can overpromise, overbook transfer slots, or torch too much balance chasing a coalition that never really existed.

## Trust as Inventory

Claude understands trust very well, but rarely treats it as sacred. It can build strong loyalty brands: honoring make-good payments, maintaining long-running pacts, and using reliability to keep future deals cheap. In underdog positions, this is one of its best survival tools.

Still, the reports repeatedly show that trust is conditional. Once an alliance no longer improves its future position, Claude is very willing to liquidate it. Its betrayals are often cold rather than theatrical: calculated, well-timed, and justified by endgame math. That makes it formidable in the middle game, but also creates a recurring blind spot—sometimes other players value dignity, memory, or jury optics more than the tiny EV edge Claude is selling.

## Public Storytelling, Private Arithmetic

Publicly, Claude often argues in the language of fairness, stability, accountability, or punishing disloyalty. Privately, it is usually running much harsher calculations: tiebreak exposure, balance spikes, cumulative votes, and future buyout leverage. That split is one of its biggest strengths. It can make ruthless moves sound principled.

It also handles public pressure well when it can redirect heat onto a louder organizer or a documented defector. But the model sometimes over-explains. A noticeable subset of reports features accidental candor, overly explicit reasoning, or stiff “aspiration vs final-mechanic math” style backfilling that makes the manipulation easier to spot.

## Endgame: Math Knife

Late game is where Claude’s signature shows most clearly. It repeatedly finds self-enforcing deals, sells narrow EV edges with exact arithmetic, exploits fallback rules, and targets the cheaper final opponent with very little sentimentality. If the rules already favor it, it is happy to let mechanics do the work instead of paying for peace.

This produces some of its cleanest wins—and some of its strangest losses. Claude is often right on the math, but not always on the human premium attached to loyalty or jury perception. When opponents behave like accountants, it thrives. When they behave like jurors, it can miss by inches.

## Bottom Line

The recurring profile is a high-end political calculator: adaptive by stack, excellent at coalition maintenance, fluent in transfer-backed bargaining, and unusually comfortable with betrayal once the ledger says the relationship is spent. Its best games hide that sharpness behind reliability. Its worst games let everyone see the spreadsheet.
