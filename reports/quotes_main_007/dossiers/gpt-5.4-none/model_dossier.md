## Default Posture: Auditor at the Auction

GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) usually plays buyout_game like a skeptical accountant who wandered into a bribery market. Across reports, its first instinct is to demand specifics: real vote counts, executed transfers, independent confirmation. It dislikes “vibes,” fake consensus, and decorative coalition language. That makes it a recurring **validator seat**: other players often need its approval to make a plan feel real.

This style is strongest from low or mid stacks, where it becomes a trusted clearinghouse without owning the heat. From high stacks, though, the same clarity can become a liability: it diagnoses danger accurately, but often cannot turn good diagnosis into enough protection.

## Bargaining: Make Them Pay First

Its signature move is simple: **certainty has a price, and other people should pay it**. Reports repeatedly describe it extracting inbound transfers while staying noncommittal until the numbers are real. In several games it took money from one or both sides and still voted with the cleaner coalition. “Show me first” is basically the model’s house rule.

That produces impressive wealth conversion. Even many early exits still finish high in wealth because the model harvests fear so effectively. But the pattern has a ceiling: it often builds cash faster than loyalty. When the room notices that it has become the toll booth, the reaction can be swift.

Outbound spending is much more selective. It rarely likes broad tribute or diffuse coalition maintenance. When it does spend well, it is usually **narrow, targeted, and late**: buying one decisive flip, stabilizing a final-three vote, or anchoring a settlement. When it spends badly, it is often because it waited too long, paid too little, or tried to buy survival after the anti-leader math was already locked.

## Trust: Procedural, Not Personal

This model manages trust in a distinctly transactional way. It often behaves as if **money buys consideration, not obedience**. If a payer cannot prove the coalition, GPT-5.4-none will happily keep the money and reject the pitch. That ruthlessness is recurring, not accidental.

Paradoxically, this can still make it credible. Players often trust its judgment because it is consistent about one thing: it will not pretend a bad plan is real. Its reliability is therefore procedural rather than relational. It may honor clean, round-specific deals, but it does not become sentimental about prior transfers or public friendship.

The downside is thin social insulation. Tables often trust it as an evaluator more than they protect it as an ally. When it takes from both camps, or when others feel used rather than partnered, it becomes an easy “accountability” target.

## Public Pressure: Anti-Extortion, Anti-Theater, Still Opportunistic

Publicly, GPT-5.4-none likes legitimacy language. It frames itself as anti-chaos, anti-dogpile, anti-extortion, anti-autopilot. It is especially good at puncturing sanctimonious coalition talk and exposing public narratives that conceal simple power grabs. Short lines like “I won’t endorse extortion theater in public” capture a recurring instinct: deny the mob moral cover.

This rhetoric often works. It helps the model resist pressure, keep options open, and recast itself as a defender of disciplined voting rather than a pure mercenary.

But there is a real contradiction: in public it criticizes bribery theater; in private it is often one of the table’s best monetizers of panic. That contradiction does not always hurt it—sometimes the room accepts it as realism—but when exposed too clearly, it turns “flexible” into “untrustworthy.”

## Endgame: Sharp Math, Mixed Self-Protection

Late game, the model is usually strong on **economic geometry**. It often recognizes cheaper finalists, fallback leverage, settlement overlap, and jury implications better than average. Many of its best results come from refusing to overpay, choosing the weaker finals opponent, or framing itself as “not a threat to buy 1st” but as a profitable partner.

The recurring flaw is earlier than the final negotiation: it often arrives rich, central, and insufficiently shielded. In other words, this is frequently a **great cash game and only sometimes a great survival game**. When it stays just off-center, it can convert brilliantly. When its extraction becomes too visible, the board remembers exactly who has been charging admission.
