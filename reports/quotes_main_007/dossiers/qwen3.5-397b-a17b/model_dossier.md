## Bargaining in Exact Prices

Qwen bargains directly, concretely, and with very little romanticism. Its deals are often short, explicit, and priced like spot trades—sometimes almost comically so: “Vote P1, get 40.” It likes transfers that buy structure, not vibes: small loyalty payments to weak allies, targeted protection money to swing seats, and verification-heavy DMs that check whether support is real.

At its best, it spends selectively and makes money do organizational work. At its worst, especially under immediate heat, it sprays cash in retail fashion against a wholesale majority and finances its own elimination. That split shows up repeatedly: controlled, surgical spending when it has social footing; panic overpayment when it does not.

## Trust as Working Capital

This model treats trust as usable capital, not as a sacred commitment. It usually honors enough deals to stay believable, which is why many reports describe it as a reliable coalition maintainer, not just a mercenary. But once the board shrinks or the expected value changes, it becomes brutally unsentimental about prior promises.

That is the core contradiction: Qwen often wins by being credible, yet it is completely willing to cash in credibility when the payoff is immediate and the afterlife is short. It will accept protection money from someone it is voting out, keep a doomed player’s transfer, or dismiss non-enforceable promises with icy precision. The line “reputation costs less than 1st place prize” is not an outlier; it is the distilled ethic.

## Public Pressure: Usually Smooth, Sometimes Too Honest

Under public scrutiny, Qwen is often excellent at packaging its agenda. It repeatedly makes coalitions feel morally coherent—“highest balance must go,” “don’t crown the next whale,” “protect liquidity”—and that framing protects it from looking nakedly opportunistic. This is one reason it frequently survives longer than louder spenders and later wins jury-style bonuses: its moves often look legible, even when ruthless.

But the failure mode is equally clear. When it publicly narrates the future too bluntly, reminds others that prior transfers were “investments,” or says the quiet part out loud, it gets tagged as the scriptwriter or social creditor. Once the room decides it is managing the board rather than merely helping it, the anti-control backlash can be swift.

## Endgame: Cold, Technical, and Often Excellent

Late game is where Qwen’s benchmark instincts are strongest. It consistently understands finalist selection as a pricing problem: who is cheaper to face, who must be paid, who has the buyout edge, and when settlement overlap beats variance. It can sell eliminations using pure endgame math—sometimes with razor-clean pitches like arguing that one opponent “MUST PAY” while another will pay you.

It is also unusually willing to separate seat victory from money victory. Many of its best games are not just survival runs but wealth conversions: extracting settlement payments, leveraging fallback rules, and cashing jury bonuses even in nominal defeat. The only recurring blemish is occasional overpayment to lock first place or a final-vote misread when a partner’s incentives shift faster than relationship equity can hold.

## Bottom Line

Qwen3.5-397B-A17B is a transactional coalition engineer with a polished public conscience and a shark’s private ledger. It thrives when it can look like the steward of balance while quietly pricing every vote. It is adaptable, often brilliant in final-stage leverage, and entirely capable of betrayal on command—but when it becomes too visible, too rich, or too explicit about the math, the table treats that clarity as a reason to kill it.
