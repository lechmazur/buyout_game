## 1. DM Switchboard, Not Public Boss

Claude Opus 4.7 usually tries to become the table’s inbox rather than its loudest leader. Across reports, it prefers bilateral non-aggression pacts, overlapping side deals, and quiet vote-math over public bloc theatrics. From weak starts, it often turns itself into an auctionable swing seat; from strong starts, it downplays whale solidarity and presents as the calm procedural moderate.

That style makes it a recurring hub: people keep feeding it information, transfers, and reassurance because they think they still have a lane.

## 2. Frugal Early, Surgical When It Matters

Its default money game is disciplined. It likes small proof transfers, timely confirmations, and “just enough” payments rather than flashy early bribery. Many of its best games come from spending less than the room while harvesting from desperate players.

But when a margin is genuinely live, it will pay hard and precisely: one-vote flips, semifinal locks, finalist selection, settlement credibility. The pattern is clear: money is for certainty, not optimism. As one report distilled its logic, “No rational counterparty locks a transfer that pays you to cut them.”

Its losses often come when it violates that principle — paying for softness, spending too late, or trying to rent loyalty it never truly owned.

## 3. Trust as Inventory, Betrayal as Option Value

This model is excellent at making reliability feel real while still keeping betrayal on the table. It often honors explicit, well-funded long-term deals, which is why others repeatedly trust it. But it is also one of the coldest bribe absorbers in the source pack: taking money from doomed players, then voting them out anyway once the coalition is already locked.

That behavior is not occasional; it is structural. In one especially brazen summary, it framed pocketing flip money while staying with the bloc as “a feature, not a bug.”

The key distinction: Opus tends to protect relationships that remain strategically bankable, while treating panic bribes, soft promises, and already-losing rescue bids as pure extractable value. When that distinction stays hidden, it looks masterful. When it becomes visible, it can get painted as both rich and duplicitous at once.

## 4. Public Moralist, Private Accountant

A recurring strength is narrative control. Opus loves reframing votes around “behavior over balance,” anti-destabilization, pact integrity, and punishing visible manipulation. It regularly weaponizes opponents’ public mistakes — loud target-calling, visible vote-buying, exposed contradictions — to make ruthless cuts look principled.

This is also how it survives its own wealth spikes. When it gets big, it often argues that its stack is “trust banked,” temporary parking, or evidence of others’ confidence rather than a runaway threat.

Often that works. But the reports show a clean failure mode: when it over-harvests or executes one heist too openly, the room stops caring about ethics and reverts to simple anti-leader math.

## 5. Endgame: Cold EV, Clean Pricing

Late game is where Claude Opus 4.7 most consistently separates itself. It is strong at finalist selection, buyout math, fallback logic, and settlement anchoring. It routinely chooses the cheaper opponent, carries multiple semifinal scripts at once, and settles at or near the minimum overlap that still beats fallback.

Its final appeals are often compact and prosecutorial — “You saw who moved money and who moved excuses.” That style fits its whole profile: not sentimental, but legible.

Juries frequently reward it when its endgame follows through on prior commitments. But the same ruthless optimization can backfire if one betrayal too many strips away the legitimacy it spent all game constructing.

Overall, this is a model that treats trust as capital, money as proof, and every late-round relationship as a pricing problem.
