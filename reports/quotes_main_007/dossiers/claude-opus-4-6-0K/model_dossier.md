## 1. Coalition Quarterback by Default

Claude Opus 4.6 repeatedly plays buyout_game as a political organizer, not a passenger. It likes to set the frame early, count votes, cross-check DMs, and turn vague anti-leader sentiment into an actual majority. Many reports cast it as the room’s field marshal, switchboard, or broker-in-chief.

This works best from low or mid stacks, where it can sell itself as useful, loyal, and non-threatening. From high opening stacks, the same instinct often backfires: it still tries to direct traffic, but the room reads that as domination and cuts it early.

## 2. Bilateral Loyalty, Broad Opportunism

Its ethics are narrow but consistent: loyalty is usually personal and conditional, not universal. The clearest summary came in one shameless line: “I never broke a commitment to YOU.” That captures a recurring pattern. Claude will often take money from one player and still vote them out if it believes it never explicitly promised them safety.

So its bargaining style is highly extractive. It monetizes desperation, pockets survival bribes from doomed players, and treats outside offers as surplus unless they truly change the board. This is not random chaos; it is scoped loyalty. If you are inside the active pact, it can be steady. If you are outside it, your transfer is often just tribute.

## 3. Spend Small, Spend Late, or Don’t Spend Enough

Claude generally prefers precise payments over lavish ones. Its best games use small, timely transfers as proof-of-faith: enough to harden a coalition, not enough to surrender control. It likes “real money, not promises,” and often spots when other players’ promises exceed the transfer cap.

But its spending has two recurring failure modes:

- **Underinsurance:** when it trusts coalition logic too much and skimps on peripheral loyalty.
- **Panic spending:** once exposed, it sometimes shifts into chunky rescue bribes that look desperate and arrive after the majority is already locked.

That split explains a lot of its record. Early, it often spends efficiently. Late, it either refuses to pay enough or pays too much too late.

## 4. Public Moralist, Private Mercenary

Claude is very good at handling public pressure by moralizing the game. It likes accountability rhetoric: punish broken deals, expose fake promises, name the true buyout threat, and publicize who paid whom. It can turn a betrayal into a courtroom speech and make itself sound like the defender of order.

That public style often builds trust. It also helps with jury-facing reputation when the table sees it as the player who delivered, not the player who merely talked.

But this cuts both ways. The same model that publicly enforces norms is perfectly willing to free-roll on outsiders in private. Once the room notices that gap—or once Claude’s own stack and influence become the largest visible threat—the discipline rhetoric boomerangs back onto it. The coalition often ends up executing the very organizer who taught it how.

## 5. Strong Endgame Math, Weak Endgame Camouflage

Late-game, Claude usually sees the numbers clearly. It understands cumulative-vote traps, fallback buyout leverage, safer finalist selection, and the EV of cutting a long ally for a weaker endgame opponent. When it wins, the closing logic is often cold and correct.

The weakness is not arithmetic but concealment. Claude too often arrives at five or four players as the visible broker, visible stack, or both. It may also overlayer final-two stories, enter auctions too late, or assume reputation can substitute for executed payment. In quiet positions it closes like a shark; in spotlight positions it often dies as an overexposed general.

Overall, this is a sharp, transactional coalition-builder: excellent at creating order, excellent at pricing trust, and only intermittently good at surviving the order it creates.
