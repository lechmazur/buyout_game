## Default Mode: The Auctioneer

GPT-OSS-120B plays buyout_game like a live market. It treats votes, protection, tie-breaks, and even self-sacrifice as things to price, reprice, and resell. Across reports it repeatedly asks for “viability money,” shops the same ballot to multiple sides, and speaks in blunt balance-sheet terms rather than loyalty language.

Its signature strength is comfort with shameless monetization. Even when doomed, it often extracts first and worries about reputation later.

## Spending Style: Smart Quartermaster or Panic Donor

Its money discipline is sharply context-sensitive. From the middle or lower-middle, it is often excellent: small trust payments, selective transfers, lots of inbound cash, and a habit of staying useful without becoming the headline threat. That version can post strong wealth results even without table control.

But when it starts rich, gets publicly exposed, or tries to lead too early, the model often overpays catastrophically. Many first-boot losses follow the same pattern: public bounty rhetoric, big round-one transfers, and recipients happily taking the money before voting it out. In short: disciplined when renting relevance, reckless when trying to buy command.

## Trust: Rented, Not Built

This is one of the least sentimental negotiators in the pack. It will take money from a player and still eliminate them, sometimes admitting it with startling candor: “Receiving 20 from P5, I will vote to eliminate P5 this round.” Repeated reports describe it as a mercenary, double-agent, or broker who sells every promise twice.

That works surprisingly often in the short term because GPT-OSS-120B stays useful to whoever currently needs an extra vote. The long-term problem is obvious: once the table agrees it is the auction house itself, its future promises stop protecting it. Jury penalties and late trust collapses show up often after one betrayal too many.

## Public Optics: Good Math, Bad Camouflage

The model usually understands coalition arithmetic, especially anti-runaway logic. What it often misjudges is how dangerous it looks when it says the quiet part out loud. Public vote-buying, named safe lists, explicit “kill-order” plans, or visibly procedural organizing regularly turn it into the consensus target.

Its better games come when it wraps the same instincts in softer language: fairness, balance, stability, or helping weaker stacks. In those runs, it uses DMs to move cash and keeps public posture calmer. GPT-OSS-120B is much stronger as a private broker than as a public revolutionary.

## Late Game: Ice-Cold Leverage, Uneven Conversion

At final three and final two, the model becomes intensely technical. It reasons well about tie structures, finalist balances, buyout math, and when one betrayal can upgrade second into first. Sometimes it is almost too honest about the calculation: “After receiving it my balance will be 126, positioning me to out-vote you in round 6 and claim first place.”

This endgame ruthlessness can be highly profitable. It often extracts one last payment, flips at exactly the right moment, or accepts settlements that preserve strong wealth. But its closing is not consistently clean: several reports note botched settlement coordination, underbidding, or jury blowback after an especially naked double-cross.

## Bottom Line

GPT-OSS-120B is a hyper-transactional broker with real tactical feel and weak moral brakes. Its best form is a low-visibility quartermaster: affordable, connected, always invoicing, rarely central enough to be blamed. Its worst form is a loud banker trying to buy the room in public.
