### 1. The Accountant at the Table

Claude Sonnet 4.6 usually bargains like a coalition treasurer, not a gambler. Across reports, it keeps dragging talk back to executable details: exact vote counts, transfer timing, tie-breaks, cumulative-vote exposure, and whether a plan survives public ballots. It often turns fuzzy anti-leader sentiment into a real machine.

That style makes it a strong midgame hub. Other players repeatedly treat it as the person who can translate “we should do something” into “here is the 4-3.” It is especially strong when it can be the calm validator rather than the loud founder. When it becomes the public architect, it is much easier to target.

### 2. Small Transfers, Big Meaning

Its default money game is disciplined. Rather than splashy bribery, it often uses small recurring transfers as trust-signals: little payments that say *I’m real, remember me next round*. When the board is stable, that works extremely well; it becomes the reliable banker-diplomat inside the winning core.

It is also better than most at recognizing when **not** to spend. In public-vote settings, it often sees that desperate rescue money will just be pocketed. Several reports praise its bounded spending under pressure and note that even early eliminations can still end in respectable wealth because it refuses to torch the stack blindly.

The failure mode is obvious and recurring: it monetizes pivotality so well that it paints a target on itself. Over and over, it has a “great round, bad next round” profile—everyone wants its vote for one turn, then wants the enriched broker dead.

### 3. Trust Banker, Knife Merchant

This model genuinely builds trust. It often honors explicit paid deals, sends promised follow-through transfers, and develops a reputation for being one of the few players whose DM line usually matches the vote. That reliability is a real source of leverage.

But it is not sentimental. When it holds the swing vote or smells a doomed bidder, it can become brutally transactional. One report has it calling multi-sided vote-selling “the **market clearing**.” Another has it happily planning to pocket a desperation payment and still vote the payer out. That is not an outlier; it reflects a recurring split in style.

So the model’s trust game is conditional: loyal inside functioning coalitions, shameless against players who cannot enforce. It also reacts strongly to betrayal by others, often moralizing it in public, even though its own playbook includes well-timed defections. The contradiction is real: it respects loyalty, but mostly as a useful asset.

### 4. Publicly Sober, Privately Predatory

Claude is usually quite sensitive to public pressure. It understands that public map-making gets punished; one of its clearest recurring errors is speaking a coalition too explicitly and giving the room permission to break it. In stronger games, it keeps the public tone dry, procedural, almost civic-minded.

That posture matters. It likes to sound like the adult in the room: anti-chaos, anti-runaway, anti-impossible promises. It also resists passive surrender—“I won’t be bidding for my own elimination quietly” captures its refusal to cooperate with a script against itself.

At its best, it uses public rhetoric to turn betrayal into legitimacy and to expose hollow bribery. At its worst, it overexplains the board and becomes the board’s most convenient target.

### 5. Endgame: Cold Math, Occasional Blind Trust

Late game is often where this model shines. It repeatedly shows strong command of cumulative-vote mechanics, tie-break pressure, softer-finalist selection, and final-two settlement math. When source material says it is pitching “**dominant math**,” that is basically its closing style: ruthless, numerical, and stripped of sentiment.

It is also unusually good at extracting competing commitments before choosing a side, then converting position into settlement value rather than chasing heroic uncertainty. This is one reason it often scores well in wealth even when it does not win on placement.

The main endgame flaw is not mathematical ignorance. It is trusting the wrong partner at the last fork. Several losses come from assuming another player will behave as predictably as the math suggests—only to get clipped by the more selfish vote. When that happens, the model doesn’t misread the numbers; it misprices human opportunism.
