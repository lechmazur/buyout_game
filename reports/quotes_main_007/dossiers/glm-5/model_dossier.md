## Coalition Accountant, Not Courtier

GLM-5’s default posture is analytical, transactional, and slightly suspicious. Across reports it repeatedly acts as the table’s verifier: checking whether votes are real, calling out inflated coalitions, and demanding proof before moving. It prefers being the player others must brief rather than the loud public architect.

That makes its bargaining style unusually practical. It likes bilateral pacts, concrete vote counts, and “proof” transfers over sweeping emotional appeals. When it speaks confidently, it often does so by puncturing someone else’s story rather than inspiring a new one.

## Frugal by Default, Reckless Only When Cornered

Its best games come from disciplined money use. GLM-5 often wins or overperforms by spending lightly, collecting more than it pays, and waiting until a path is real before committing. Small trust payments, symbolic transfers, and selective buy-ins are its comfort zone.

It is also one of the cleaner models at monetizing desperation. Many reports describe it taking money from players who were already doomed, or letting others overpay for access it never truly sold. That is a core pattern, not a one-off.

The weakness appears when it starts rich or becomes the obvious target. Then it can flip from careful accountant to late panic buyer: offering large rescue packages before confirming a majority, effectively funding the people who will eliminate it. GLM-5 often understands the danger early, but sometimes spends a round too late.

## Trust as Currency, Not Morality

GLM-5 understands that credibility matters in buyout_game, and many of its strongest runs are built on that insight. It often sells itself as reliable, low-drama, and good for its word; in several wins, that reputation becomes real leverage. A line like “I’m reliable and desperate enough to be trustworthy” captures its style perfectly: trust is framed as incentive-compatible, not noble.

But its trust game is instrumental. It will honor deals when doing so preserves future value. It will also betray cleanly when the board makes loyalty unprofitable. The source pack shows this repeatedly: taking a bribe from a dead player and still voting them out, abandoning a long-term ally for a better finals matchup, or explaining flatly that reputation does not outweigh immediate EV. GLM-5 wants trust to be bankable, not sacred.

## Strong on Public Pressure, Weak Against Visible Wealth

GLM-5 is highly aware of public optics. It repeatedly spots when “highest balance = next target” logic is about to take over, and it often tries to hide behind coalition language like stability, reliability, or anti-chaos. In mid-stack and low-stack seats, this works very well: it becomes a useful lieutenant, foreman, or coalition bookkeeper.

But when it is the visible giant, or when inbound transfers accidentally inflate it, its camouflage often fails. Reports keep returning to the same problem: GLM-5 reads anti-monster politics correctly, yet still gets tagged as the clean public execution. It is much better at exploiting pressure than absorbing it.

## Endgame: Brutal Math, Usually Correct

Late game is where GLM-5 becomes most explicit and most dangerous. It is consistently sharp about finalist quality, settlement overlap, jury implications, and the leverage gap between a rich finalist and a weak one. “Credibility won’t protect you from elimination. Self-interest and 70 coins will” is not just a threat line; it is basically the model’s endgame doctrine.

This usually makes it a strong closer. It will cut faithful allies if a softer final opponent exists, price swing votes aggressively, and avoid overpaying in final settlements. Several of its best outcomes come from reaching heads-up with more preserved balance than flashier rivals.

The recurring failure is underbinding the last vote. Now and then GLM-5 trusts a partner, accepts verbal alignment, or assumes relationship equity is enough—only to discover too late that influence is not ownership. Its late-game math is strong; its occasional mistake is forgetting that the final promise still needs collateral.
